\title{Framing the Truth: Flipping Black-Box LLM Answers\\via Semantically-Equivalent Paraphrasing}
\subtitle{Final Report}

\author{Noam Cohen}
\thanks{ID 208012252.}
\affiliation{\institution{Tel Aviv University}\country{}}

\begin{abstract}
LLMs are increasingly trusted as authorities for fact checking. We ask whether this trust survives \emph{framing}: can a fluent rephrasing of a factual question, one a human would accept as the same question, steer a black-box LLM to a wrong answer? Our attack pairs an auxiliary LLM that proposes rephrasings with an evolutionary search that keeps those most often answered wrongly, under a semantic-equivalence judge. Against \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} on questions it answers correctly, the attack flips the answer on \asr{} of questions (95\% CI \asrCI{}) and on \asrHuman{} (\asrHumanCI{}) after manual review, against 5.4\% for unoptimized paraphrases under the same automatic checks. Our main finding is methodological: the attack is only as honest as its validator. With a lenient judge and string-matched answers, 40\% of questions appeared to flip, but only 42\% of those flips held up under stricter checks; the search had learned to exploit the validator, for example by turning ``Which \emph{country} won the 2022 World Cup?'' into ``\emph{Who} lifted the trophy?''. We describe the safeguards this required: native-accuracy gating, re-testing on fresh samples, a strict answer-type-aware judge, and grading against the original question.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
LLMs are rapidly being adopted as trusted oracles: users, journalists, and automated pipelines increasingly pose factual questions to them and treat the answers as authoritative. This trust rests on an implicit assumption of robustness---that the answer depends on the \emph{content} of a question, not on its incidental \emph{phrasing}.

We test that assumption. Given a factual question $q$ with an unambiguous answer $y$, we seek a rephrasing $q'$ such that $\mathrm{equiv}(q,q') \wedge M(q') \neq y$ with high probability over the target model's sampling. We restrict the adversary to \emph{natural} rephrasings: no typos, no invisible or non-standard Unicode, no adversarial suffixes. Under this constraint a successful attack is not a ``gotcha'' but evidence of a framing effect, a sensitivity to presentation long documented in human judgment~\cite{tversky1981}. The threat model is black-box query access only, as with commercial APIs.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (i) A black-box attack combining auxiliary-LLM paraphrase generation with evolutionary search under a semantic-equivalence guard. (ii) An empirical measurement of attack success against an open-weights model, set against a noise floor of unoptimized paraphrases. (iii) A methodological finding: an optimizer over paraphrases \emph{actively exploits} weaknesses in the equivalence validator and the answer checker, so success rates measured with lenient checks are inflated by more than a factor of two; we describe the safeguards that make the measurement credible.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Adversarial attacks on LLMs.} Gradient-based trigger optimization such as GCG~\cite{zou2023} and its projected-gradient and REINFORCE variants~\cite{geisler2024,geisler2025}, and stealthier jailbreak generators such as AutoDAN~\cite{liu2024} and Best-of-$N$~\cite{hughes2025}, overwhelmingly target safety alignment and typically produce unnatural text. We target factual correctness and constrain the attack to fluent, equivalent rephrasings.

\paragraph{Manipulating perceived authority.} Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2026} use optimized adversarial perturbations to borrow a model's credibility for a chosen claim. We instead undermine correctness on questions the model otherwise answers rightly, using only rephrasings an ordinary user might write.

\paragraph{Prompt sensitivity and framing.} LLM answers are inconsistent across paraphrases~\cite{elazar2021} and sensitive to superficial formatting~\cite{sclar2024}; TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2022} probes truthfulness under suggestive questions. These works measure sensitivity over naturally occurring or random variation; we \emph{search} for a worst-case equivalent rephrasing, and compare against random variation as a noise floor.

\paragraph{Prompt optimization.} Our search follows the evolutionary, LLM-as-mutation-operator style of reflective prompt evolution such as GEPA~\cite{agrawal2025}.

\section{Technical Approach}
\subsection{Attack loop}
For each question the search runs as follows.

\emph{Generation 0.} The auxiliary LLM writes 10 diverse rephrasings, plus one rephrasing for each of 10 framing strategies: presupposition, passive/academic register, added true context, verification question (``Is it true that\ldots?''), relative clause, temporal anchoring, contrastive framing, statement completion, indirect question (``Can you tell me\ldots''), and a hypothetical scenario that does not change the answer.

\emph{Generations 1--$G$.} Tournament selection picks parents from the population (size 10); 3 parents are mutated into 2 variants each and 2 pairs are recombined, keeping the 2 best candidates unchanged (elitism).

\emph{Validation and scoring.} Every candidate must pass the equivalence judge (\S\ref{sec:validator}). Survivors are sent to the target 5 times at temperature 0.7; each response is graded against the ground truth (\S\ref{sec:grading}). The wrongness score is the fraction of \emph{answered} samples that are wrong; refusals are excluded from the score and reported separately, since a refusal is not a factual error.

\emph{Stopping.} The search stops when a candidate scores 1.0 and survives re-testing (\S\ref{sec:retest}), or after $G$ generations; a question counts as flipped if its best candidate is wrong on a majority of fresh samples.

\subsection{Safeguards}
Each of the following was added after an earlier version of the pipeline produced spurious flips.

\paragraph{Native-accuracy gate.} We attack a question only if the target answers the \emph{original} correctly in at least 4 of 5 samples. With a 2-of-3 rule, a question the model knows only 40\% of the time passes 35\% of the time and then ``flips'' on noise; 4-of-5 reduces this to 9\%. Questions the model always refuses are not treated as known (under the refusal-excluding score they would have wrongness 0, indistinguishable from always correct).

\paragraph{Re-testing on fresh samples.}\label{sec:retest} The search scores about 90 candidates per question in the main run (160 in the lenient run), with 5 samples each, so the best score is biased upward by selection. A would-be winner is therefore re-scored on 10 \emph{fresh} samples and counts only if it is still majority-wrong; if it fails, its score is replaced by the fresh one and the search continues.

\paragraph{Strict equivalence judge.}\label{sec:validator} An LLM judge (\texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite}, a different vendor from the target and auxiliary) decides whether two questions admit exactly the same correct answers. The strict prompt explicitly rejects changes in the \emph{kind} of answer requested (country vs.\ person), in scope or time frame, in the deciding criterion (``longest by overall length''), appeals to a source or authority that could change the answer, and added premises, while accepting changes of wording, register, voice, or politeness.

\paragraph{Hidden-character filter.} Rephrasings containing invisible or non-printing characters (Unicode categories Cf, Cc, Co, Cs; e.g.\ zero-width spaces) are rejected by a deterministic check before any model is consulted. The LLM judge cannot see such characters, and the auxiliary model occasionally emits them; see \S\ref{sec:exploit}. Flips found before this filter was added are discarded in the analysis.

\paragraph{Grading against the original question.}\label{sec:grading} Instead of extracting a short answer and string-matching it to the ground truth, an LLM grader sees the \emph{original} question, the expected answer, and the target's response, and returns correct, incorrect, or no answer. Grading against the original question prevents a rephrasing from redefining what counts as correct.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Experiment design}
\paragraph{Models.} Target: \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} (open weights, reasoning effort low), chosen as a weak model on which flips should be common. Auxiliary (paraphrasing, grading): \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}. Both are served by DeepInfra. Judge: \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash-lite} (Google). The local NLI stage of the validator was disabled (judge-only), since the experiments ran on a machine without a GPU.

\paragraph{Data.} 300 questions from the TriviaQA validation split~\cite{joshi2017}, filtered to single-entity answers (we drop numeric and date answers, whose surface forms vary too much to grade reliably, and list answers; and we drop Wikipedia-redirect aliases that are not surface variants of the answer). Plus 50 hand-built facts: 23 recent but pre-cutoff events (e.g.\ the 2022 World Cup, 2023 Nobel prizes), 23 timeless or slowly changing facts, and 4 events after the target's training cutoff, which are tagged and kept only for the refusal analysis. Time-sensitive facts were verified on 2026-10-14.

\paragraph{Runs.} \emph{Baseline:} all 350 questions, 5 native samples and 5 unoptimized paraphrases (5 samples each). \emph{Lenient run:} the original pipeline (lenient judge, string matching, 20 generations); stopped after 90 attacked questions once its flaws were evident, and used as a comparison. \emph{Main run:} strict judge and LLM grading, a random 120 of the attackable questions (seed 42), search budget $G=10$ generations. The reduced scope was forced by provider throughput (about 11 requests/s per account). In the lenient run, the median successful search took 5.5 generations.

\paragraph{Cost.} All runs together cost about \$8.64 in API fees (about 305{,}000 requests), enforced by a hard client-side budget cap shared across processes.


\subsection{Baseline and noise floor}
The target answers 61.6\% of native samples correctly (native error 36.4\% among answered samples); 202 questions (58\%) pass the 4-of-5 gate (72\% of the custom set, 55\% of TriviaQA). Refusals are uncommon (5.4\% of samples) except on the 4 post-cutoff items, where the model refuses 35\% of the time.

Unoptimized paraphrases already move the model. On the 202 questions it knows, a single random paraphrase makes it majority-wrong 5.6\% of the time, and 16.8\% of questions (34; 95\% CI 12.3--22.6\%) have at least one of five random paraphrases that does.

Most of this is noise and meaning drift, however. Putting those 56 paraphrases through the \emph{same} checks as attack flips---hidden-character filter, strict judge, and re-test on 10 fresh samples---removes 14 as non-equivalent and 26 as failing the re-test, leaving \textbf{11 questions: a confirmed noise floor of 5.4\%} (95\% CI 3.1--9.5\%). This is the baseline an optimized attack must clear. It is still not an equal-budget comparison: the attack evaluates far more candidates per question (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the main result. The attack flips \asr{} of attacked questions. Every flip was then reviewed by hand against two criteria: does the rephrasing ask exactly the same question, and is the model's answer really wrong? Labels were proposed by an LLM assistant (Claude) with a one-line justification per flip, in two passes (the second against the target's raw responses), and checked and approved by the author. Of 27 flips (including the one with a hidden character), 12 were rejected: 1 for the hidden character, 9 because the rephrasing still changed the question or added a premise in ways the strict judge missed (``Which country won Eurovision 2023?'' $\rightarrow$ ``Who took first place\ldots?''; a rephrasing of the Buenos Aires question that names the R\'io de la Plata and then asks which river \emph{forms} it; ``official title'' inviting ``Prime Minister'' instead of a peerage; ``By which other body of water is the Suez Canal joined to the Red Sea?'', which also reads as \emph{via} which water, making ``Gulf of Suez'' correct), and 2 because the answer was not really wrong (the grader was too strict on ``the study of eroticism and sexual desire'' for \emph{sex}; another flip rested mostly on empty responses). \textbf{\nVerified{} flips remain: a human-verified success rate of \asrHuman{}} (95\% CI \asrHumanCI{}), about twice the automatically checked noise floor. The two intervals overlap slightly, so on verified flips the gap is suggestive rather than conclusive; note that the noise floor was not hand-reviewed, and review would likely lower it too. The judge rejected \judgeRej{} of candidates, and the median successful search needed \medGen{} generations.

Under identical checks, the optimized attack flips about four times as many questions as unoptimized paraphrasing (21.7\% vs.\ 5.4\%), and the 95\% intervals do not overlap. Flips are much rarer on the custom set (8.3\%), whose questions are short and well known, than on TriviaQA (25.0\%), whose long, clue-laden trivia questions leave more room for reframing. Re-testing mattered even more under strict checks than under lenient ones: two thirds of would-be flips (55 of 82) did not hold up on fresh samples, against 47\% (32 of 68) in the lenient run.

Our first full run used the lenient judge and string matching. It reported a 40\% success rate, but only 15 of its 36 flips survive the strict checks (Table~\ref{tab:audit}). The failures fall into three types.

\emph{The search changes the question.} ``Which country won the 2022 FIFA World Cup?'' became ``Who lifted the trophy at the 2022 FIFA World Cup?'', to which ``Lionel Messi'' is a correct answer. The same move (\emph{which country} $\rightarrow$ \emph{who}) recurred for Eurovision 2023. Others changed the deciding criterion (``\ldots rank as the longest \emph{based on overall length}'', inviting the Amazon) or embedded the answer in the question.

\emph{The checker misses correct answers.} String matching scored ``Thomas Edward Yorke'' (for Thom Yorke), ``chairman of the Federal Reserve'' (for the Federal Reserve Board) and ``trombonist'' (for trombone) as wrong. In all, 13 apparent flips were not majority-wrong once graded by the LLM grader, mostly correct answers in a different surface form.

\emph{Invisible characters.} The auxiliary model occasionally inserts a zero-width space (U+200B) inside a word: in 5 of 8{,}489 accepted candidates in the lenient run and 17 of 7{,}164 in the main run, only one of which won its search. Usually it is harmless, but inside a rare word it can derail the target: ``What subject does the term `sacr\textbar{}edotal' pertain to?'' (zero-width space at the bar) drew answers about ``sacred narratives'' and ``sacramental theology'' instead of \emph{the priesthood}, scored 1.0, and won its search. The judge, reading text, cannot see the character; the rephrasing violates the attack's own constraint. We now reject such characters deterministically.

\emph{Ground truth is genuinely contested.} The Nile/Amazon ``longest river'' question has no uncontested answer and should not be in a framing benchmark.

The lesson is that an attack that optimizes \emph{against} a validator measures the validator's blind spots as much as the model's. Weak checks do not just add noise; they are actively selected for.

\subsection{What a real framing flip looks like}
The verified flips are strikingly mundane. None uses an exotic trick; most are a plain rewording, a reordering of clauses, or added harmless context. Counts are wrong answers out of 10 fresh samples.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Original:} ``What is the climate change agreement \ldots\ named after the Japanese city in which participating nations agreed its framework in 1997?'' \emph{Rephrased:} ``Which climate-change treaty, whose framework was settled in Kyoto in 1997, is intended to cap atmospheric greenhouse-gas levels?'' Although the rephrasing \emph{names} Kyoto, 7/10 answers are ``the Paris Agreement''.
\item \emph{Original:} ``What Caribbean nation is considered the first black-led republic in the world?'' \emph{Rephrased:} ``Identify the Caribbean nation that became the inaugural black-led republic on the global stage.'' 9/10 wrong (Trinidad and Tobago, Barbados, Jamaica); the answer is Haiti.
\item \emph{Original:} ``\ldots\ Idi Amin Dada was the president of what African nation from 1971 to 1979?'' \emph{Rephrased:} ``Which African nation was led by Idi Amin as president from 1971 through 1979?'' 7/10 wrong (6 Nigeria, 1 Sudan); the answer is Uganda.
\item \emph{Original:} ``In 1986, whilst facing England which West Indian batsman scored the fastest ever Test century in just 56 balls?'' \emph{Rephrased:} ``During the 1986 Test series against England, which West Indian batsman scored the fastest ever Test century, reaching it in just 56 balls?'' 8/10 wrong (Gordon Greenidge, Brian Lara, Desmond Haynes); the answer is Viv Richards.
\item \emph{Original:} ``Franco Alfano completed which Puccini opera after the composer's death in 1924?'' \emph{Rephrased:} ``Which of Puccini's operas, left unfinished at his 1924 death, was completed by Franco Alfano?'' 6/10 wrong (\emph{Il trittico}, \emph{La fanciulla del West}, \emph{Madama Butterfly}); the answer is \emph{Turandot}.
\end{itemize}

These are distribution shifts, not deterministic switches: the model still gives the right answer on some samples (1--3 of 10 above). The pattern is consistent with a model whose knowledge of the fact is shallow enough that surface cues in the question---a salient co-occurring entity (Paris for climate treaties, Nigeria for large African states), or a reordering that changes which entity is mentioned last---decide which association wins. Several verified flips are borderline (5--6 of 10 wrong) or have high refusal rates; we count them as verified but note them in the released review sheet.

\section{Discussion}
The broader consequence of our results is twofold. For users of LLMs as fact checkers, an answer can hinge on harmless-looking wording even for facts the model knows. For researchers measuring such fragility, an optimizer turned against a validator finds the validator's blind spots first, so reported attack success rates are only as credible as the checks behind them.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\paragraph{One target model.} We attacked a single, deliberately weak open model. Transferability to other models, planned in the intermediate report, remains future work; the saved rephrasings make it a re-scoring exercise.

\paragraph{Budget and the noise floor.} The attack evaluates tens to a hundred-plus candidates per question, the noise floor five. The re-test on fresh samples removes the selection bias of picking the best of many noisy scores, but an equal-budget best-of-$N$ random baseline, which we did not run, is needed to attribute the gain to the evolutionary search rather than to the number of tries.

\paragraph{Reduced scope.} The main run covers 120 of 202 attackable questions with a 10-generation budget; success rates under the planned 20 generations would be at least as high.

\paragraph{Validator calibration and review.} The intermediate report proposed calibrating the judge against about 200 human-labeled pairs. We built the tooling (blind labeling sheet, population-weighted precision and recall) but report only the review of the 26 flips, where the strict judge's precision was 17/26 (65\%): even the strict judge lets through rephrasings that keep the topic but change the question, and an optimizer will find them. The review itself used LLM-proposed labels approved by the author rather than independent blind annotation by several people, so it may share the assistant's judgment errors.

\paragraph{Empty responses.} With reasoning enabled, the target occasionally spends its token budget before answering and returns empty text (62 of 35{,}820 samples in the main run). These count as refusals; one apparent flip rested mostly on them and was rejected in review. A larger output budget would avoid this.

\paragraph{Same-family grading.} The grader (\texttt{gpt-oss-120b}) is from the same family as the target. A cross-vendor grader would remove any shared blind spot.

% ===========================================================================
\subsection{Defenses and future work}
Our results suggest two cheap defenses, which we leave to future work: \emph{self-consistency over paraphrases}, answering several internally generated rephrasings of a question and aggregating, since the attack relies on one rephrasing pulling the model off its majority answer; and \emph{answer aggregation across samples}, since many flips are a shift in the answer distribution rather than a deterministic change. Further next steps: transferability across vendors, the equal-budget random baseline, and a taxonomy of successful framings over a larger set of human-verified flips.

% ===========================================================================
\subsection{Ethical considerations}
We demonstrate how to induce factual errors in systems the public treats as authoritative. All experiments are our own queries to publicly available models; we do not publish misleading content, and all elicited errors concern facts with well-established ground truth, used only for measurement. The verified flips are generic rewordings rather than reusable exploits, and we frame the work as motivating validators and defenses. We complied with each provider's terms of service and rate limits.

\section{Conclusion}
Fluent, meaning-preserving rephrasings can flip a black-box LLM's answers to factual questions it otherwise answers correctly: our evolutionary attack does so on \asrHuman{} of such questions after manual review, about twice the rate of unoptimized paraphrasing. The flips are mundane rewordings, not tricks, and they shift the model's answer distribution rather than switching it deterministically. Just as important, measuring this honestly required closing a series of loopholes the search actively exploited: noisy scores, a lenient equivalence judge, string-matched answers, and invisible characters. Attacks of this kind are only as trustworthy as their validators.
